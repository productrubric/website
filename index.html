Judge the work against the same standard.

ProductRubric helps product teams make better decisions about the work in front of them. Whether a design is ready to ship, whether a survey can be trusted, whether findings are strong enough to act on: those calls usually rest on whoever happens to review the work, using whatever criteria they improvise in the moment.

It is an open library of evaluation rubrics that replaces the improvisation. Your AI assistant applies a rubric to the work itself, a screen, a design board, a study plan, a questionnaire, or a readout, and returns a structured review, the same shape of review in Claude, ChatGPT, Grok, Cursor, or any other tool, every time.

Survey Review. 12 questions reviewed: 1 invalidating, 8 biasing, 1 burden, 2 sound.

Q1 (Invalidating): “How much do you love using Ledgerline?” presupposes the feeling it claims to measure, so the survey’s headline number is manufactured. Suggested revision: “How satisfied are you with Ledgerline so far?”, on a balanced scale.

Q6 (Biasing): “Do you agree that our pricing is unfair for freelancers?” plants its conclusion. Suggested revision: “How fair do you think Ledgerline’s pricing is for freelancers?”

Highest impact fix: replace Q1. As written, the result the team will quote most is the one the question invented.

The idea

What is a rubric?

A rubric is a review written down before the work arrives: the criteria to judge against, the scoring to use, and the format the verdict comes back in. Product teams already work from rubrics informally, in critique habits, heuristic evaluation, and survey methodology. Those traditions are frameworks. A rubric takes a framework, an established one or your own, and turns it into instructions an AI can follow consistently, so the standard survives the person who brought it.

Each rubric here is one plain Markdown file, and each one also states plainly what it cannot judge.

Who it is for

Who runs a rubric, and when

Product designers

Run one on a screen or a flow before a crit, before a design review, or at handoff, and walk in already knowing where the work is weak and where it holds up.

UX researchers

Run one on the study plan before recruiting starts, on the questionnaire before it fields, and on the readout before it goes to stakeholders. The cheap moment to catch a leading question is before anyone has answered it.

Product managers and product owners

The ship decision, and the call on whether findings are strong enough to act on, usually land on your desk. Run the same rubric the team ran and you see the design or the readout the way its toughest reviewer would, before the meeting where the decision gets made.

Product marketers

Run one on the positioning before messaging is built on it, on the messaging before the homepage and the deck repeat it, on the launch plan while it can still change, and on the battlecard before a rep trusts it in front of a buyer.

Design and research leads

Your standards stop living in your head and in old feedback threads. Written down as rubrics, they are applied the same way in every review, whoever runs it and whichever AI they use.

Small teams and solo builders

The criteria a staff designer or a research lead would apply, available in the room even when there is no staff designer or research lead in it. If you are also the one building, that covers the Accessibility Review on the interface you shipped and the Copy Review on the error messages and empty states nobody was assigned, because on a small team that person is you.

Product design

Run one before a design ships, before a crit, or at handoff. Select a rubric for its full description.

Usability Heuristics Review Standard Evaluates a screen or flow against the ten usability heuristics published by Jakob Nielsen.

Evaluates a screen or flow against the ten usability heuristics published by Jakob Nielsen, reporting problems with severity ratings.

When to use it: A structured usability inspection of a screen or flow by its design alone, the classic heuristic evaluation, when no users are available yet or before testing with users is scheduled.

What you provide: Screenshots of the screen or the flow's screens in order, or a description detailed enough to name the states, messages, and actions. State the product's context (what it is, who uses it) when the screens do not make it obvious.

How it reports: No overall score; every problem rated 0-4 on the severity scale defined in the Scoring section

Design Principles Score Standard Scores a screen or flow against seven product design principles.

Scores a screen or flow against seven product design principles, with findings tied to visible elements.

When to use it: A general design critique of a screen or flow, when you want a scored review rather than open-ended comments.

What you provide: A screenshot of the screen or flow, or a description that names the visible elements and their text.

How it reports: 1-5 per principle; overall 0-100

Inclusive Design Audit Standard Audits a screen or flow against five inclusive design principles for cultural fit.

Audits a screen or flow against five inclusive design principles for cultural fit, checking who the design includes and who it unintentionally leaves out.

When to use it: An inclusion review of a screen or flow: representation, cultural fit, access, language, and user control, judged from what is visible in the design.

What you provide: A screenshot of the screen or flow, or a description that names the visible elements, imagery, and text.

How it reports: 0-2 per principle; overall 0-10

Accessibility Review Standard Reviews a screen’s design for accessibility problems visible in a screenshot or description.

Reviews a screen’s design for accessibility problems that are visible in a screenshot or description, judged against criteria derived from WCAG 2.2.

When to use it: At design time, before a screen is built, to catch the accessibility problems a picture of the design can already show, so they are fixed in the design instead of discovered in the code.

What you provide: A screenshot of the screen at a realistic size, or a description that names the text, its colors, and the controls. State the platform (web, iOS, Android, desktop) when it is known, because target and text expectations differ by platform.

How it reports: No overall score; each finding rated Blocker, Serious, Moderate, or Minor; criteria that cannot be judged from the input are reported as Cannot judge

Copy Review Standard Reviews every piece of text on a screen and suggests a better revision for each one.

Reviews every piece of text on a screen and returns a revision for each one that fits the same space and does the same job better.

When to use it: Before design handoff, when a screen’s words need a dedicated pass: headlines, buttons, labels, empty states, and error text that were written in the layout and never reviewed as writing.

What you provide: A screenshot of the screen, or the screen’s text supplied directly (a copy list, a transcript of the design, or the strings from the product). If text is supplied directly, say so in the result.

How it reports: None

Five Second Test Simple Predicts what a first-time visitor remembers after five seconds, and what they miss.

Predicts what a first-time visitor will remember after seeing a screen for five seconds, and which notable elements they will miss.

When to use it: A first-impression check on a landing page, a hero section, an app screen, or any single screen whose job is to land its message and its main action within seconds.

What you provide: A screenshot of one screen at its full bounds, or a description that names the visible elements and their text. One screen per run.

How it reports: Memorability 0-10; simulated panel of 5; 3-of-5 rule

Attention Prediction Simple Predicts the three places a viewer’s eye lands first on a screen.

Predicts the three places a viewer’s eye will land first on a screen, in order, with positions a tool can draw as an overlay.

When to use it: To check whether a screen’s visual hierarchy sends attention where the design intends, especially when something decorative might be out-pulling the headline or the main action.

What you provide: A screenshot of the screen (required for coordinates). Optionally, the intended attention path: the elements the design wants seen first, second, and third. A text description alone can support the ranked prediction but not the coordinates; say so and omit them.

How it reports: Exactly 3 hotspots; intensity High 70-100, Medium 40-69, Low 20-39

Design Quality Deep Reviews a design board with its rationale against seven dimensions of design quality.

Reviews a design submission, a multi-frame board with its rationale, against seven dimensions of professional design quality, the way a design leader would in a critique.

When to use it: Before a design critique, a handoff, or a portfolio review, when the question is not only whether the screens work but whether the work itself is good: well aimed, well grounded, well built, and well explained.

What you provide: The design as a multi-frame board (a board overview plus the frames in reading order), with whatever rationale the designer has written on the board or alongside it. A single screen can be submitted, but the dimensions that judge intent and evidence will usually be unjudgeable from a screen alone.

How it reports: 1-5 per dimension; overall 0-100 across the dimensions that could be judged

UX research

Run one before a study is fielded, or before findings are presented to stakeholders. Select a rubric for its full description.

Study Plan Review Deep Reviews a study plan before it is fielded and judges whether it is ready.

Reviews a research study plan before it is fielded, judging whether the questions are answerable, the method fits them, and the participants and materials will produce evidence the team can act on.

When to use it: Before recruiting begins for an interview study, a usability study, a diary study, or any planned piece of product research, when the cost of fixing the plan is still low and the cost of fielding a flawed one is not yet paid.

What you provide: The study plan as written: its goals or research questions, the method, the participant definition and screening approach, and whatever session structure, tasks, or materials the plan specifies. A short informal plan is a valid input; the review judges what is there against what the study needs.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Ready, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall readiness verdict

Survey Review Standard Reviews a questionnaire question by question and rewrites the flawed ones.

Reviews a survey questionnaire question by question, finding the wording, scale, and ordering flaws that bias answers or make them impossible to give, and suggesting a corrected version of each flawed question.

When to use it: Before a survey is sent, while the questionnaire can still be changed: for satisfaction surveys, screening surveys, product feedback surveys, and any questionnaire whose results will be quoted as evidence.

What you provide: The full questionnaire text: the introduction shown to respondents, every question with its answer options and scales in order, and any skip logic described. The survey’s purpose and audience, when known, sharpen the review; without them the review judges craft alone and says so.

How it reports: No overall score; every flaw rated Invalidating, Biasing, or Burden, with a suggested revision for each flawed question

Findings Review Standard Reviews a readout and checks whether every claim traces to evidence.

Reviews a research readout, the report or summary stakeholders will read, judging whether every claim traces to evidence, whether observation and interpretation are kept apart, and whether recommendations stay proportional to what the study can support.

When to use it: Before a research readout is circulated: the findings deck, the summary document, or the report a team will use to decide something, when the question is whether a reader can trust what it claims.

What you provide: The readout itself, in full: claims, quotes, numbers, and recommendations as the audience will see them. Access to the underlying evidence (session notes, recordings, response data) strengthens the review when available; without it, the review judges the readout’s internal support and labels every claim check accordingly.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Holds, Needs work, or Missing, plus a claim-by-claim audit and a reliance verdict

Competitive Analysis Review Standard Reviews a competitive analysis: the set, the evidence, and honest strengths.

Reviews a competitive analysis, judging whether the competitor set is complete, the evidence is first-hand and dated, the comparison serves the reader’s decision, and competitor strengths are reported as honestly as their weaknesses.

When to use it: Before a competitive analysis is presented or used to set strategy, pricing, or roadmap priorities, when the document will be believed, quoted, and planned from, and the question is whether its picture of the market was assembled honestly enough to bear that weight.

What you provide: The competitive analysis as written: the competitor set, the comparison, and the conclusions drawn from them. Source material behind the analysis (product trials, pricing pages, customer reviews, win and loss interviews) strengthens the review when supplied; without it, the review judges the evidence the document presents and says so.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Holds, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall reliance verdict

Product management

Run one before a team commits: to building, to a roadmap, or to a date. Select a rubric for its full description.

PRD Review Deep Reviews a requirements document before a team builds against it.

Reviews a product requirements document before a team builds against it, judging whether the problem is established with evidence, the scope is decidable, success is measurable, and the undecided things are labeled as undecided.

When to use it: At the point a PRD, spec, or feature brief is handed to design and engineering to build from, or is sent upward for approval to build: after the thinking is written down and before the building starts.

What you provide: The PRD as written, including its goals, scope, and success measures sections in whatever form the team keeps them.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Ready, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall readiness verdict

Roadmap Review Standard Reviews a roadmap for outcomes, strategy traceability, and honest dates.

Reviews a product roadmap, judging whether it is organized by outcomes rather than outputs, whether each item traces to a strategy, whether its dates are honest about their certainty, and whether what is not on it is as clear as what is.

When to use it: Before a roadmap is shared beyond the product team: the planning cycle review, the board or stakeholder presentation, the sales enablement handoff, or the moment dates on it are about to be quoted to customers.

What you provide: The roadmap as presented: its items, groupings, and dates or horizons in whatever form the team keeps them, board, document, or slides.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Holds, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall reliance verdict

Problem Statement Review Simple Reviews a problem statement: a specific person, evidence, and no smuggled solution.

Reviews a problem statement or problem brief, judging whether it names a specific person, rests on evidence, states the cost of the status quo, stays free of a smuggled-in solution, and draws its own boundary.

When to use it: At the start of discovery or a design challenge, before solution work begins, and at intake, when a team or a stakeholder brings a problem to be worked on.

What you provide: The problem statement as written, a sentence, a paragraph, or a one-page brief.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Holds, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall verdict on whether the statement is ready to work from

User Story Review Simple Reviews a user story and its acceptance criteria against INVEST.

Reviews a user story and its acceptance criteria, judging whether the story is valuable to a named user, small and independent enough to build, estimable from what is written, and confirmed by criteria a tester could actually check.

When to use it: At backlog refinement or sprint planning, before a story is estimated or pulled into a sprint, and when a story keeps bouncing back from development or testing with questions.

What you provide: One user story with its acceptance criteria, or a small set of stories from the same feature.

How it reports: No numeric score; each story judged against each criterion, with an overall readiness verdict per story

Project Plan Review Deep Reviews a project plan before anyone commits to its dates.

Reviews a project plan before anyone commits to its dates, judging whether its outcome and scope are decidable, its milestones are verifiable states, its estimates take the outside view, its critical path is visible, its risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies are kept as a living register, and it knows how it will change.

When to use it: Before a project plan is committed to: dates promised, teams booked, stakeholders told. Also at any major replan, when the question is whether the new dates deserve more belief than the old ones did.

What you provide: The project plan as written: goal, scope, milestones, timeline, owners, and risk material.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Ready, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall commitment verdict

Marketing

Run one before positioning, messaging, a launch, or a battlecard reaches the people who will repeat it. Select a rubric for its full description.

Positioning Review Standard Reviews positioning against the five components of effective positioning.

Reviews a positioning document against the five components of effective positioning, judging whether the competitive alternatives are the real ones, the unique attributes are actually unique, the value has proof, the best-fit customer is specific, and the market category is chosen rather than defaulted into.

When to use it: When positioning is being set or reset: a positioning document or canvas, a category decision, or the input that messaging, sales decks, and a homepage will all be built from, when the question is whether the positioning itself holds together.

What you provide: The positioning document as written: the components in whatever form the team keeps them, canvas, doc, or deck section.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Holds, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall verdict

Messaging Review Standard Reviews a messaging document: insight first, proof attached, a clear ask.

Reviews a messaging document, judging whether it leads with an insight rather than the product, engages the alternatives honestly, lets the differentiated value carry the hierarchy, attaches proof at the right strength, keeps the customer as the subject, and answers objections before closing with a clear ask.

When to use it: When a messaging guide, message hierarchy, or pitch narrative is about to become the words a homepage, a sales deck, and a sales team all repeat: before it is published or rolled out.

What you provide: The messaging document as written: core message, pillars or value propositions, and proof points, in whatever form the team keeps them.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Holds, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall verdict

Launch Plan Review Deep Reviews a launch plan: tier, audiences, readiness, and rollback.

Reviews a product launch plan, judging whether the launch tier is named and resourced to match, every audience has a message and a moment, internal teams are enabled rather than surprised, execution is sequenced and owned, success is instrumented before launch day, and risk, rollback, and a slipping date are all planned for.

When to use it: Weeks before a product or feature launch, while the plan can still change: when the question is whether this launch will land as one coordinated event or as a release the market barely notices.

What you provide: The launch plan as written: goals, tier, timeline, assets, owners, and channel activities.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Ready, Needs work, or Missing, with a launch readiness verdict

Battlecard Review Simple Reviews a battlecard: claims sourced and dated, usable mid-call.

Reviews a competitive battlecard, judging whether its claims about the competitor are sourced and dated, whether the competitor's strengths are stated honestly, whether the card arms a rep for both winning and losing ground, whether it can be used in the middle of a live call, and whether its freshness is owned.

When to use it: Before a battlecard is published to the sales team, and whenever one is refreshed: when the question is whether a rep can trust what it says in front of a buyer who may know the competitor better than the card does.

What you provide: The battlecard as written: competitor overview, differentiators, talk tracks, objection handling, pricing notes, and proof points.

How it reports: No numeric score; each criterion judged Holds, Needs work, or Missing, with an overall verdict

A rubric standardizes the questions, the criteria, and the shape of the report. It cannot standardize the judgment inside the answers, which depends on the model you run and the material you give it. Treat a result as a structured review by a capable reader, not as a measurement.

FAQ

Questions

What can I use this for?

Getting a proper review of product work before it goes out, from the AI assistant you already use. Designers run rubrics on screens and flows before a critique. Researchers run them on study plans and surveys before anything fields, while the fixes are still cheap. Product managers run them on PRDs, roadmaps, and project plans before the team commits to them. Marketers run them on positioning, messaging, and launch plans before they reach the market. Every run comes back in the same shape: what holds up, what needs work, and what to fix first.

Is ProductRubric an AI tool that reviews my work for me?

No. ProductRubric contains no AI. It is a library of written standards. Your own AI assistant does the reviewing, applying the rubric you choose, and the material you evaluate never passes through ProductRubric.

How is this different from asking an AI for a critique directly?

A direct ask improvises the criteria in the moment, so the same work gets a different review every time. A rubric fixes the criteria, the scoring, and the report format in writing, so reviews are consistent, comparable across tools and across time, and honest about what they could not judge.

Can my team track whether our work is improving?

Yes. Every report ends with a Rubric summary: the rubric and its version, the date, the artifact, and the result tallied in the rubric’s own terms, criteria at each grade, flaws at each rating, any gate criteria not met. Keep the summaries across runs and the pattern shows itself: the same criterion failing less often, readiness verdicts arriving with fewer fixes, fewer biasing questions per survey. One run is one structured judgment and carries the noise of one; the trend across runs is the signal.

Which AI assistants does it work with?

Any assistant that can read text or connect to an MCP server, including Claude, ChatGPT, Grok, and Cursor. The product design rubrics need an assistant that can see images. The UX research, product management, and marketing rubrics judge documents, so any text assistant can run them.

Do I need an account or an API key?

No. The hosted MCP server is open, the files are on GitHub, and there is nothing to sign up for.

Does ProductRubric see my designs or my data?

No. The server only sends rubric text out. Your screenshots, boards, and documents stay in your own chat with your own AI provider.

What does it cost?

Nothing. The rubric files are licensed CC BY 4.0 with credit to their authors, and the server code is Apache 2.0.

Can I write a rubric for my own team?

Yes. The format is documented in the repository. You can run your own rubrics locally through the same MCP server, install them as Skills, or propose them to this library.
